Infections in pregnancy

Generally accepted practice. Confirm with obstetric and infectious diseases advice and local protocols.

Generally acceptable

  • Penicillins (amoxicillin) and cephalosporins (ceftriaxone, cefalexin)
  • Azithromycin; paracetamol for pain and fever
  • Fosfomycin or nitrofurantoin for UTI per local resistance (avoid nitrofurantoin near term)
  • Oseltamivir for influenza, started early
  • IV artesunate for severe malaria in all trimesters

Avoid or use only with specialist advice

  • Tetracyclines (doxycycline) and fluoroquinolones
  • NSAIDs, especially from 20 weeks
  • Trimethoprim in the first trimester; aminoglycosides unless essential
  • Ribavirin and high-dose fluconazole
  • Live vaccines (MMR, varicella)

Screen and act

  • Screen and treat asymptomatic bacteriuria
  • Syphilis, HIV and hepatitis B screening at booking, with treatment to protect the baby
  • Admit pyelonephritis, severe pneumonia, dengue, malaria and sepsis
  • Vaccinate: influenza, Tdap, COVID-19 per national guidance, hepatitis B if non-immune
  • Rubella or varicella exposure: seek urgent advice

Immunology essentials

What matters in clinic: when to suspect an immune problem, and how immune status changes infection risk and management.

Innate immunity

  • First line, fast, non-specific: skin and mucosal barriers, neutrophils, macrophages, NK cells, complement, interferons.
  • Deficits: neutropenia (bacterial and fungal infection), complement deficiency (Neisseria infections).

Adaptive immunity

  • T cells: coordinate response, kill infected cells. Deficits: opportunistic viral, fungal and mycobacterial infection (HIV, transplant, steroids).
  • B cells and antibodies: encapsulated bacteria and sinopulmonary infection when deficient.

Who is immunocompromised?

  • HIV with low CD4, transplant recipients, chemotherapy
  • Biologics (anti-TNF, rituximab), long-term steroids
  • Asplenia, diabetes, CKD, cirrhosis, malnutrition

Anaphylaxis: give intramuscular adrenaline first, call for help, position supine with legs raised, then give fluids and adjuncts.

Antimicrobial stewardship

Resistance is a patient-safety issue in every clinic. Five habits reduce harm.

1. Diagnose first

Is this bacterial? Most acute cough, sore throat without Centor features, and uncomplicated diarrhoea do not need antibiotics. Take cultures before antibiotics when sepsis is possible.

2. Choose the narrowest effective drug

The WHO AWaRe grouping helps: prefer Access antibiotics first-line, use Watch agents only for defined indications, and keep Reserve agents for resistant infections.

3. Dose and duration

Dose for site, weight and kidney function. Use the shortest evidence-based course; many infections need 5 to 7 days or fewer.

4. Review at 48 to 72 hours

Check cultures and response. Narrow, switch from IV to oral, or stop. Document indication and stop date.

5. Counsel the patient

Explain why antibiotics are not needed for viral illness, give safety-net advice, and discourage self-medication and leftover antibiotics.

Vaccination and prevention

Check vaccination status at every contact. Follow your national immunisation schedule (for Pakistan, the EPI schedule).

Adults at higher risk

  • Annual influenza vaccine
  • Pneumococcal vaccine for age, diabetes, chronic lung, heart, kidney or liver disease, asplenia, immunosuppression
  • Hepatitis B for health workers, diabetes, CKD, close contacts
  • Tetanus booster every 10 years and for wounds
  • COVID-19 per current national guidance

Live vaccine caution

Avoid live vaccines (MMR, varicella, BCG, oral polio, yellow fever) in pregnancy and in severe immunosuppression. Give inactivated vaccines before starting immunosuppression when possible.

Health worker protection

  • Hand hygiene, correct PPE, safe sharps handling
  • Hepatitis B immunity confirmed
  • Needle-stick: wash, report immediately, assess for HIV PEP and hepatitis B and C follow-up
